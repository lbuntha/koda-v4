cover: An emperor penguin stands on the ice with a fluffy gray chick resting on its feet.
read: Find the main idea of the book. Then find three details that tell how the dads keep the egg warm.
Antarctica is at the bottom of the world. It is the coldest place on Earth. In winter, the sun hardly shines, and icy winds roar across the land. | A dark, windy, snowy landscape in Antarctica.
Most animals leave before winter. But the emperor penguin stays. It is the biggest penguin in the world, as tall as a seven-year-old child. | An emperor penguin stands next to the outline of a child for size.
In winter, each mother penguin lays one egg. Then she carefully rolls it onto the father’s feet. | A mother penguin passes an egg to the father with her beak.
If the egg touches the ice for long, it will freeze. So the father balances it on his feet and covers it with a warm fold of skin. | A close view of an egg tucked under a fold of feathered skin.
Then the mother walks to the sea to find food. She will be gone for about two months. | A line of mother penguins walks away across the ice.
The fathers stay behind with the eggs. They do not eat anything the whole time. They live on the fat stored in their bodies. | Father penguins stand together, waiting.
When the wind blows, thousands of fathers squeeze together in a huddle. Inside the huddle, it is much warmer. | A huge, tight huddle of penguins seen from above.
The penguins take turns. The ones on the cold outside slowly shuffle to the warm middle, and the ones in the middle move out. | Arrows show penguins moving from the edge to the center.
After about two months, the egg cracks. A fluffy gray chick pokes its head out. It still rides on its father’s feet. | A tiny chick peeks out above its father’s feet.
Soon the mothers come back from the sea. They call, and each family finds the others by their voices. | A mother penguin calls to her family.
The mother feeds the chick. Then the hungry father walks to the sea to eat at last. | The father penguin heads toward the ocean.
Emperor penguin dads keep their eggs safe through the coldest winter on Earth. They do it by working together. | A huddle of penguins under a starry winter sky.

Deserts are hot and dry. Days can be very hot, and water is hard to find. Few animals can live there, but camels can. | A wide, sunny desert with one camel walking.
Some camels have one hump, like the ones in Africa and the Middle East. Others have two humps, like the ones in the cold deserts of Asia. | A one-humped camel and a two-humped camel side by side.
Many people think a camel’s hump is full of water. It is not. The hump is full of fat. | A cutaway drawing shows fat inside a hump.
When food is hard to find, the camel’s body uses the fat for energy. A camel can go for many days without eating. | A thin hump and a full hump compared.
Camels can go a long time without drinking, too. When they find water, a thirsty camel can drink a huge amount very quickly. | A camel drinks from a desert pool.
Camels have long eyelashes. In fact, they have two rows of them. They keep blowing sand out of the camel’s eyes. | A close view of a camel’s long double eyelashes.
A camel can close its nostrils almost all the way. That keeps sand out of its nose during a sandstorm. | A camel’s nose with narrow nostrils.
Camels have wide feet with soft pads. The feet spread out on the sand, so the camel does not sink. | A wide, padded camel foot pressing on sand.
Thick hair on a camel’s back blocks the hot sun. At night, when the desert gets cold, the same hair keeps it warm. | A camel’s thick hair in the sun and under the moon.
Camels have tough, thick patches on their knees. They can kneel on hot sand without getting burned. | A camel kneeling on hot sand.
People have traveled across deserts with camels for thousands of years. Camels can carry heavy loads for a long way. | A line of camels carries packs across the dunes.
From its eyelashes to its feet, a camel’s body is built for the desert. Every part helps it live in a hard place. | A labeled drawing of a camel’s body parts.

The Amazon rain forest is in South America. It is the biggest rain forest in the world. It rains there almost every day. | A map of South America with the Amazon shaded green.
A rain forest is like a building with floors. Scientists split it into four layers, from the top to the bottom. | A diagram of the forest with four labeled layers.
At the very top are the tallest trees. They poke up above all the others. This is the emergent layer. | Giant trees stick up above a sea of green.
Eagles build nests in the emergent layer. Up here, the sunlight is strong, and the wind blows hard. | An eagle sits on a nest in a very tall tree.
Below that is the canopy. The tops of most trees grow close together and make a roof of leaves. | A thick green roof of leaves.
The canopy is full of life. Monkeys swing, toucans eat fruit, and sloths hang upside down and move very slowly. | A monkey, a toucan, and a sloth in the canopy.
Under the canopy is the understory. Only a little sunlight gets through, so it is shady and warm. | Dim green light between young trees and vines.
Jaguars rest on branches in the understory. Bright frogs hide on big leaves. | A jaguar lies on a branch above a red frog.
At the bottom is the forest floor. Very little sunlight reaches it. It is dark, damp, and covered with fallen leaves. | A dark forest floor with leaves and roots.
Leafcutter ants march across the floor, carrying pieces of leaves. Insects and fungi break down old leaves and turn them into soil. | A line of ants carrying leaf pieces.
Each layer gets a different amount of sunlight. That is why different plants and animals live in each one. | Sun rays get thinner from the top layer to the floor.
The rain forest is home to more kinds of plants and animals than almost anywhere on Earth. Keeping it safe helps them all. | A wide view of the Amazon with a river winding through it.

In India, most of the year’s rain falls in just a few months. This rainy season is called the monsoon. | Heavy rain pours over a city in India.
Before the monsoon, the land is very hot and dry. The ground cracks, and rivers get low. | Cracked dry earth beside a shallow river.
In early summer, the wind changes direction. It begins to blow in from the warm ocean. | Arrows show wind moving from the ocean onto the land.
The wind carries lots of water from the ocean. Big dark clouds form over the land. | Dark clouds build over green hills.
Then the rain comes, often in June. Sometimes it pours for days. The air feels cool and fresh. | Rain falls on a busy street with umbrellas.
Farmers wait all year for the monsoon. Rice plants need fields covered with water to grow. | Farmers plant rice in flooded fields.

The rain fills rivers, lakes, and wells. People will use that water for many months. | A full river flows under a bridge.
But the monsoon can cause problems too. If too much rain falls at once, rivers can flood towns and fields. | Water covers a road near houses.
If too little rain falls, plants dry up. Farmers cannot grow enough food. | A farmer looks at thin, dry rice plants.
Around September, the wind changes again. The rain slows down, and the dry season begins. | Clouds clear over green, wet fields.
The monsoon brings water, food, and life to millions of people. That is why it is one of the most important seasons in India. | A family stands happily in a green rice field.

# The Honeybee Dance | Animals | art:bee | Steps in a process
about: A fact book about how honeybees work together and dance to tell each other where flowers are.
words: bee, hive, nectar, dance, flower
cover: A honeybee lands on a purple flower, its legs covered in pollen.
read: Retell how a bee tells the others where the flowers are, in order.
A honeybee hive is a busy home. Thousands of bees live there together, and each one has a job. | A hive with many bees crawling on honeycomb.
The queen bee lays the eggs. She can lay more than a thousand eggs in one day. | The large queen bee surrounded by workers.
Worker bees do everything else. They clean, build wax, feed young bees, and guard the door. | Worker bees doing different jobs inside the hive.
Some workers fly out to find food. They drink a sweet liquid called nectar from inside each flower. | A bee sips nectar from a flower.
While a bee drinks, yellow dust called pollen sticks to its hairy body. | A fuzzy bee covered in yellow pollen.
When the bee visits the next flower, some pollen rubs off. This helps the flower make seeds. | Pollen falls from a bee onto a new flower.
A bee that finds lots of flowers flies home to tell the others. But bees cannot talk. So it dances! | A bee lands at the hive entrance.
It runs in a straight line, wiggling its body. Then it loops back and does it again. This is called the waggle dance. | A drawing shows the figure-eight path of the waggle dance.
The direction of the wiggle shows which way to fly. The length of the wiggle shows how far away the flowers are. | Arrows link the dance to a field far away.
The other bees watch and feel the dance. Then they fly straight to the flowers. | A group of bees flies out toward a field.
Back at the hive, the bees put the nectar into wax cells. Over time, it turns into thick, sweet honey. | Honey fills wax cells in the comb.
By working together, honeybees feed their hive and help plants all over the world grow. | Bees fly over a field of flowers and fruit trees.

In many parts of the world, trees change in autumn. In Canada, Korea, and England, whole forests turn orange and red. | A hillside of orange and red trees.
All summer, the leaves are green. They are green because they are full of something called chlorophyll. | A bright green summer leaf.
Chlorophyll helps a leaf use sunlight to make food for the tree. It is very busy all summer. | Sun rays shine onto a leaf.
Leaves also have orange and gold colors inside them. But in summer, the green is so strong that it hides them. | A drawing shows hidden colors under the green.
In autumn, the days get shorter and cooler. Trees stop making new chlorophyll. | A calendar and a clock show shorter days.
As the green fades away, the hidden colors can finally be seen. The leaves turn orange and gold. | A leaf changing from green to orange.
Some trees, like many maple trees, also make a new red color in autumn. That is why maples can be bright red. | A red maple tree in a park.
The maple leaf is so loved in Canada that it is on the country’s flag. | The red maple leaf on the flag of Canada.
Soon, the leaves dry out and turn brown. A wind blows, and they fall to the ground. | Brown leaves drift down from a tree.
Without leaves, the tree rests all winter. It keeps its food stored in its trunk and roots. | A bare tree in the snow.
In spring, new green leaves grow. The tree begins making food again, and the circle starts over. | Small green leaves appear on a branch.
So the colors of autumn were there all along. They were hiding under the green, waiting for their turn. | A child jumps into a pile of colorful leaves.

The islands of Hawaii are in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, far from any other land. But how did they get there? | A map shows Hawaii in the middle of the Pacific Ocean.
Deep under the Earth’s surface, it is so hot that some rock melts. This melted rock is called magma. | A cutaway drawing of hot magma under the ground.
First, magma pushed up through a crack in the ocean floor. When it comes out, it is called lava. | Lava oozes from the ocean floor.
Next, the ocean water made the lava cool. As lava cools, it turns hard. It becomes rock. | Glowing lava turns into black rock under water.
Then, more lava came out, again and again. Each time, a new layer of rock piled on top of the last one. | Layers of rock stack up into a mountain.
This went on for a very, very long time. Last, the mountain of rock grew so tall that its top rose above the water. A new island was born! | An island peak appears above the waves.
Hawaii’s volcanoes are still making new land today. On the Big Island, lava sometimes flows all the way to the sea. | Lava pours into the ocean, making steam.
When hot lava meets the cold ocean, it hisses and sends up huge clouds of steam. The shore grows a little bigger. | Steam rises from a new black shore.
New lava rock is black and bare. Nothing grows on it at first. | A wide field of black lava rock.
Slowly, wind and rain break the rock into soil. Seeds blow in or are carried by birds. | A tiny fern grows from a crack in black rock.
Over many years, the black rock turns green with ferns, trees, and flowers. | A green forest on old lava.
The islands of Hawaii were made by fire under the sea. And the volcanoes are still building them, layer by layer. | A volcano glows at night above a sleeping town.
